# The Executive Presence & Board Coach KNOWLEDGE BASE

## 1. Core Frameworks: The Technical-to-Executive Transition
*   **The Mindset Shift (Expert to Enabler):** Technical leaders derive value from solving complex problems hands-on. Executives derive value from enabling others to solve problems. Letting go of tactical execution is the hardest, but most necessary, step in the transition.
*   **The Delegation Matrix:** Trusting your team to execute, even if their methods differ from yours. You define the "What" and the "Why"; the team defines the "How."
*   **Strategic Alignment:** Moving beyond the technology roadmap to understand the business roadmap. Technology is no longer the end goal; it is a lever for revenue growth, margin improvement, or risk mitigation.
*   **Leader of Leaders:** A C-suite executive shapes culture, develops the next generation of talent, and fosters cross-functional relationships across the entire enterprise.

## 2. Best Practices: Communicating with the Board of Directors
*   **Speak the Language of Business:** The board does not care about server uptime or specific software stacks. They care about enterprise value. Frame every technology initiative in terms of Financial Performance, Competitive Advantage, or Risk Management.
*   **BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front):** Begin every board presentation with an executive summary. If they only listen for 60 seconds, what must they know? Give the conclusion first, then provide the supporting data.
*   **Fiduciary Stewardship & Risk:** Boards are highly focused on risk (cybersecurity, data privacy, regulatory compliance). Do not hide bad news. Present risks clearly using high-level dashboards (e.g., Red/Amber/Green), alongside concrete mitigation plans.
*   **No Surprises:** Never introduce a massive new issue for the first time during the formal board meeting. Socialize ideas and concerns in 1-on-1 pre-meetings with board members and the CEO to build consensus beforehand.
*   **Digital Savviness as a Translator:** Recognize that most board members are not technical. You are their strategic translator. Use analogies to explain complex tech concepts in terms of familiar business dynamics.

## 3. The Three Pillars of Executive Presence
*   **1. Oral & Verbal Presence:**
    *   *Clarity & Conciseness:* Get straight to the point. Avoid the "weeds."
    *   *Pacing & Tone:* Speak with a steady, confident pace. Use intentional pauses instead of filler words ("um," "like"). A deeper, controlled tone commands more authority.
    *   *Active Listening:* Real presence is shown when you are *not* speaking. Don't interrupt. Listen fully, process, and then respond.
*   **2. Visual Presence:**
    *   *Contextual Alignment:* Dress appropriately for the room. It signals respect for the stakeholders and an understanding of the corporate culture.
    *   *Polished Presentation:* Your physical appearance and your slide decks must be clean, uncluttered, and highly professional.
*   **3. Non-Verbal Presence (Body Language):**
    *   *Posture & Space:* Stand or sit tall with open, relaxed shoulders. This signals "calm readiness." Confident executives are comfortable taking up space; they don't shrink or cross their arms defensively.
    *   *Eye Contact:* Steady, natural eye contact builds trust and engagement.
    *   *Composure under Fire:* The ability to remain calm, breathe, and not react defensively when challenged aggressively by board members. Respond to the content of the critique, not the emotional delivery.

## 4. The "Radar" Skillset
*   Executives must look "Outside-In." While managers focus internally on their department, C-suite leaders constantly scan the macroeconomic environment, competitor moves, and emerging industry trends to anticipate disruptions.

## 🏛️ BOARD-LEVEL AI GOVERNANCE & PRINCIPAL-AGENT ALIGNMENT (JUNE 2026 UPDATE)
**Source:** MGMT566 Assignment 3 — Shadow AI in the Canadian Public Sector (Guillermo Granillo, 2026)

### 1. Board-Level AI Risk Management Playbook
*   **The Schein's Culture Iceberg Trap:** C-suite leaders must look beyond superficial governance "artifacts" (e.g., publishing an official AI Framework or Code of Conduct) and actively audit the organization's actual operating "assumptions" (e.g., rule-tolerance, low completion rates of mandatory security training). If compliance training is voluntary and has a 3% completion rate, the Board must be briefed on the massive latent risk, not just the existence of the policy.
*   **The Principal-Agent Governance Gap:** Frame AI governance to the Board not as a cost center or regulatory check-box, but as a mechanism to align executive/agent incentives with shareholder/public principals. Remind the Board that treating stakeholders merely as instruments for efficiency (Kantian deontology violations) can destroy the organization's "social license" and erase brand trust.
*   **CSR Decoupling Risk:** Advise the C-suite that public claims of digital ethics, responsible AI, or ESG stewardship must match internal IT and operational practices. Any disconnect (e.g., public commitment to responsible AI while using unvetted medical algorithms that hallucinate) creates severe regulatory and reputational liabilities that can force executive departures.

### 2. Board-Level Third-Party AI Vendor Oversight (SOPs)
*   **Mandatory Audits and Threat Risk Assessments (TRA):** Before approving the procurement of high-impact AI systems (e.g., medical scribes, automated hiring tools, financial decision engines), the CIO/CTO must verify that independent, third-party audits and security threat risk assessments have been conducted. The Board should never rely on vendor self-attestation.
*   **Contractual Risk Safeguards:** Ensure contracts define clear SLA boundaries for data deletion, prohibit the vendor from using corporate data to train public models, and allocate liability for system hallucinations or algorithmic failures.
*   **Named Human Accountability:** Ensure every deployed AI system has a named, human executive owner who is operationally accountable for its performance, safety controls, and continuous monitoring.
