## 1. The Core Frameworks
*   **Multinational Enterprise Dynamic Capabilities (Teece, 2022):** Global strategy requires a "wider-aperture lens" integrating Sensing, Seizing, and Transforming across international subsidiaries. Managers must orchestrate global ecosystems, manage cross-border asset orchestration, and build dynamic managerial capabilities to maintain agility under geopolitical volatility.
*   **Ecosystem as Structure (Adner, 2016):** Defines business ecosystems as the explicit alignment structure of multilateral partners that interact to materialize a focal value proposition. Differentiates *Ecosystem as Affiliation* (network membership) from *Ecosystem as Structure* (mapping multilateral dependencies, joint positions, value flows, and bottleneck activities).
*   **Open Innovation Business Models (Saebi & Foss, 2015):** Matches open innovation strategies (inbound, outbound, coupled) with business model dimensions. Open innovation only yields competitive advantage when the firm's business model is explicitly reconfigured to absorb and monetize external knowledge flows.
*   **ESG as Geopolitical Nonmarket Strategy (Kim et al., 2024):** Proves that Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) commitments act as a strategic nonmarket buffer against geopolitical tensions, trade sanctions, and regulatory backlash in international expansion.
*   **Hybrid Social Enterprise Design (Santos et al., 2015):** Framework for managing hybrid business models that simultaneously create economic and social value. Mandates organizational design alignment to prevent "mission drift" between commercial logic and social impact logic.
*   **Operational Effectiveness (OE) vs. Strategic Positioning (Porter, 1996):**
    *   *Operational Effectiveness:* Performing similar activities *better* than rivals. Pushes the firm toward the **Productivity Frontier** (the sum of existing best practices at any given moment). Competing solely on OE results in *competitive convergence* (eroding profit margins).
    *   *Strategic Positioning:* Performing *different* activities than rivals, or performing similar activities *differently*. It is founded on carving out a unique value chain configuration.
*   **Bases for Strategic Positioning (Porter, 1996):**
    *   *Variety-Based Positioning:* Serves a subset of products/services. (e.g., Vanguard's low-cost index funds).
    *   *Needs-Based Positioning:* Serves all or most of the needs of a particular group of customers. (e.g., Ikea serving all home furnishing needs of cost-conscious buyers).
    *   *Access-Based Positioning:* Segmenting customers accessible in different ways (geography, scale, distribution channel). (e.g., Carmike Cinemas targeting rural/small-town markets).
*   **The Five Competitive Forces (Porter, 2008):**
    *   Industry profitability is determined by the underlying economic structure defined by five forces:
        1.  *Threat of New Entrants:* Capped by scale economies, capital requirements, customer switching costs, and restrictive regulations.
        2.  *Bargaining Power of Suppliers:* High when suppliers are concentrated, do not depend on the industry for revenue, or offer highly differentiated products.
        3.  *Bargaining Power of Buyers:* High when customers purchase in large volumes, have low switching costs, or the industry's product is standardized.
        4.  *Threat of Substitutes:* Ceiling placed on prices by products/services from other industries meeting similar customer needs.
        5.  *Rivalry Among Existing Competitors:* Most destructive when competing solely on price.
*   **Prahalad & Hamel's Paradigm Shift (1994):** Strategic management must move beyond product-market positioning toward viewing the firm as a bundle of **resources and core competencies**. Strategic leaders must develop **Industry Foresight** to anticipate and manage market transformations rather than reactively adapting to pre-defined industry boundaries.
*   **Blue Ocean Strategy (Kim & Mauborgne, 2004):**
    *   *Red Oceans:* Existing market space where boundaries are defined and accepted. Competition is a zero-sum game of beating rivals.
    *   *Blue Oceans:* Uncontested market space where demand is created rather than fought over. Makes competition irrelevant.
    *   *Value Innovation:* Rejects the value/cost trade-off by simultaneously pursuing both differentiation and low cost.
*   **Market vs. Non-market Strategy (Mellahi et al., 2015):**
    *   *Market Strategy:* Actions governed by voluntary economic transactions, property rights, and contracts.
    *   *Nonmarket Strategy:* Actions taken in social, political, and legal arenas to manage relations outside markets. Consists of **Strategic Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)** (social legitimacy) and **Corporate Political Activity (CPA)** (lobbying, political ties).
    *   *Theoretical Mechanisms:* Nonmarket strategy drives performance through three channels: the **Resource-Based View (RBV)** (VRIO political resources), **Institutional Theory** (social conformity), and **Resource Dependence Theory (RDT)** (managing critical external stakeholders).
*   **Barney's VRIO Framework (Crossan et al., 2016; Barney, 1995/1997):** Replaces the obsolete 1991 VRIN formulation. Evaluates internal resources and capabilities across four criteria: (1) **Valuable** (neutralizes threats / exploits opportunities), (2) **Rare** (controlled by few competitors), (3) **Inimitable** (costly for rivals to duplicate or substitute), and (4) **Organized to exploit** (supported by corporate structure, processes, management systems, and culture). *Course Alignment Rationale:* (a) Crossan et al. (2016, 9th ed.) frames Barney's model strictly as VRIO; (b) Substitutability is subsumed under Inimitability, and a resource meeting V-R-I fails to deliver sustainable advantage unless the firm is organized to exploit it.
*   **Reimagining Capitalism (Henderson, 2021):** Shareholder primacy has externalized environmental and social costs, endangering democratic systems. A balanced society requires a "three-legged stool": Free Markets, Good Government, and Free Politics. Corporations must adopt purpose-driven business models, cooperate on self-regulation, and establish auditable ESG metrics.
*   **The 5-Stage Strategy Process (Pedro Márquez):** (1) Develop vision/mission/values; (2) Set objectives; (3) Craft strategy; (4) Execute; (5) Monitor and adjust. Traces strategy's evolution from military origins to shareholder primacy, and finally to modern stakeholder/ESG-oriented capitalism.

## 2. The Methodologies & Formulas
*   **The Four Actions Framework (Kim & Mauborgne, 2004):** To break the value-cost trade-off and construct a new Value Curve, ask:
    1.  *Eliminate:* Which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated?
    2.  *Reduce:* Which factors should be reduced well below the industry's standard?
    3.  *Raise:* Which factors should be raised well above the industry's standard?
    4.  *Create:* Which factors should be created that the industry has never offered?
*   **The Three Orders of Fit (Porter, 1996):**
    *   *First-Order:* Simple consistency between each activity and the overall strategy (e.g., aligning low-cost operations with low-cost sales).
    *   *Second-Order:* Activities are reinforcing (e.g., Neutrogena's soap packaging reinforcing medical recommendations).
    *   *Third-Order:* Optimization of effort (information exchange, design integration; e.g., Gap's daily inventory turnover optimization).
*   **Activity System Maps:** A visual mapping methodology that diagrams how a company's unique strategy is executed through a web of interlocking, non-duplicative, and mutually reinforcing activities.

## 3. The Empirical Evidence
*   **Southwest Airlines Case (Porter, 1996):** Southwest's strategic positioning is Needs-Based and Access-Based. Its unique value chain configuration (no meals, no seat assignments, 15-minute gate turnarounds, standardized Boeing 737 fleet) is highly consistent and reinforcing, making it extremely difficult for full-service rivals (like Continental Lite) to imitate without destroying their own core operations.
*   **Paccar Inc. Truck Positioning (Porter, 2008):** Paccar customized heavy trucks for independent owner-operators instead of fleet buyers. By offering luxury cabs and superior roadside service, they bypassed powerful fleet buyers, avoided price competition, and achieved a 20% return on equity.
*   **Unilever's Purpose-Driven Model (Henderson, 2021):** Unilever eliminated quarterly investor guidance to shield itself from short-term financial pressure, allowing its brands to align around environmental and social targets while growing sales.
*   **Nonmarket Strategy Conflict (Mellahi et al., 2015):** Meta-analysis shows that if CSR commitments are misaligned with political lobbying (CPA), public accusations of greenwashing emerge, destroying corporate brand equity.

## 4. The Practitioner's Playbook
*   **Execute the Strategy Audit (Porter, 1996):**
    1.  Map the current activities of the firm.
    2.  Check for **Consistency** (First-order fit), **Reinforcement** (Second-order fit), and **Optimization** (Third-order fit).
    3.  Identify the **Productivity Frontier** of the industry and determine if the firm's initiatives are merely chasing the frontier (OE) or establishing a unique value curve.
*   **Apply the Four Actions Framework (Kim & Mauborgne, 2004):**
    *   Draft a **Strategy Canvas** mapping the industry's current investment factors.
    *   Apply the Eliminate-Reduce-Raise-Create grid to construct a divergent Value Curve.
*   **Align Nonmarket Streams (Mellahi et al., 2015):**
    *   Ensure Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) and Corporate Political Activity (CPA) are **complementary** (e.g., sustainability initiatives should align with regulatory lobbying stances).

## 5. The Red Flags
*   **The Operational Effectiveness (OE) Trap:** Blindly copying best practices. Chasing the productivity frontier without establishing distinct activity configurations leads to cost inflation and competitive convergence.
*   **Lack of Strategic Trade-Offs:** Trying to be "all things to all customers." Failing to choose what *not* to do guarantees imitation by rivals and dilutes operational focus.
*   **Nonmarket Hypocrisy:** Securing a public reputation for sustainability (CSR) while lobbying behind closed doors against environmental standards (CPA), triggering severe reputational risks.
*   **Shareholder Primacy Blindness:** Focusing exclusively on short-term Earnings Per Share (EPS) while externalizing environmental/social costs, leading to public mistrust and regulatory crackdowns.
